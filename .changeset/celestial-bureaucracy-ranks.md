**The Celestial Order gets a civil-service ladder, because its own note says the clergy are clerks.**

Tëngvōk Vān Lëi and its bureaus carried Excommunicate → Hierophant, with a Sacristan, an Almoner
and a Lector, over a body that states plainly: _"It is not a faith in the Western sense… The
Celestial Order is an administration"_ and _"The temple is not a church — it is a **celestial post
office**. The priests are not pastors — they are **clerks** who know the correct forms."_

Ranks now run **Struck from the Rolls** → **Petitioner** → **Copyist** → **Clerk** → **Seal-Holder**
→ **Registrar** → **Intendant** → **Chief Intendant**, on `meritocracy` rather than `theocracy`,
with rank lore drawn from the civil ladder — apprentice, clerk, officer, magistrate. Level 0 is
administrative non-existence rather than damnation: the ledger closed, no temple will stamp for
them, and no ancestor can advocate for a descendant who cannot be addressed.

Offices are the bureaucracy's own: the **Tëng Güng** (Celestial Minister) whose power is the
authority of the office and not of their person, the **Vënsōk Zhük** who audits a moral ledger line
by line at death, the household **Zātkür** that files the annual report, and on the mortal side the
Seal-Holder, Registrar, Advocate before the Supreme Administrative Court, Receiver of Tribute and
Assessor of Sacrifice. Rëkgüng additionally gets its own bureau: the **Lëknëi** (Lightning Clerk)
who processes strike orders and the **Lüngzhük** (River Administrators) who execute them.

**Sacrifice as Postage** is added as doctrine. A sacrifice is not a bribe or a gift — it is
carriage, and it scales with how far up the administration a document must travel: a dead parent
is reachable from the home censer with food and written prayers; a village's elder ancestor needs a
shrine or a temple; a named god needs a temple and a significant tribute in coin and kind. Failure
is therefore diagnosable — underpaid, misaddressed, burned at the wrong censer, outranked by a
competing claim, or filed by someone whose ledger could not support it — and the Tānvüri work
through that list before it occurs to anyone that the god declined.

The censer is treated as apparatus rather than an office: a household keeps a small one, a village
temple has one, and a great temple keeps many — one per bureau it is licensed to address, since a
petition burned at the wrong censer reaches the wrong bureau and is lost.

The other **16** Tengvok affiliations still carry the generic ladder; this establishes the pattern
for them.
