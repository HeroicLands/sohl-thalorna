# Appearance {#appearance}

Hotep stands 5'9" tall with a heavy build — broad-shouldered and thick through the chest, the frame of a man who was never slight and who has spent three years walking the empire's roads. He has dark brown skin, a shaved head (maintained by habit long after the temple's purification codes ceased to bind him), and dark eyes set deep beneath a heavy brow. His features are blunt and serious: a prominent nose, a strong jaw, full lips that settle naturally into an expression of watchful gravity. The most striking marks on him are the ritual tattoos on both forearms — the feather-of-Má'át inked in ceremonial blue — scored through with deliberate cuts, the temple's mark of expulsion. He keeps his left palm turned inward when he can; the horizontal branding scar there is the mark they burned into him the day they cast him out.

He dresses in a plain cloth tunic and worn leather sandals — indistinguishable from a hundred other travelers on the river roads, which is precisely the point. The only concession to his former station is a walking staff of blackwood, unadorned but well-made, and the way he carries himself: shoulders squared, head level, moving with the deliberate economy of a man who learned to command rooms before he learned to survive roads.

# Dossier {#dossier}

Hotep was born in [[doc-ankhsetun|Ankh-Setûn]], the second son of a minor scribe who managed grain inventories for the nome's central granary. The family was respectable but unremarkable — literate, employed, and utterly without social ambition. His father's highest aspiration for his sons was that they follow him into the scribal service. Hotep's older brother did exactly that. Hotep, at the age of nine, was accepted as a Wab into the Temple of Má'át at Ankh-Setûn — not through family connections but because the temple's recruiting priests identified a boy with an unusual capacity for concentration and an instinctive discomfort with dishonesty that bordered on the pathological.

The temple schools of Má'át are among the most rigorous in the empire. Where the schools of Íšét teach diplomacy and the reading of people, and the schools of Thōth teach the preservation and interpretation of knowledge, the schools of Má'át teach law, procedure, the weighing of evidence, and the sacred obligation of truthful speech. A Wab of Má'át learns to adjudicate disputes, administer oaths, conduct the rituals of judgment, and — above all — to recognize deception. The training is not gentle. Students are tested relentlessly: presented with false evidence, subjected to elaborate fabrications designed to test their ability to separate truth from persuasion, and punished not for being deceived but for failing to admit it. The theology is simple and unforgiving: Má'át is the principle that holds the cosmos together. Untruth weakens it. A priest who cannot identify falsehood — or worse, who tolerates it — is not merely incompetent but cosmically dangerous.

Hotep thrived. He was never the most brilliant student — that distinction belonged to others who went on to scholarly careers in the capital — but he was the most dogged. Where quicker minds leaped to conclusions and occasionally leaped wrong, Hotep worked through evidence methodically, tested his assumptions against procedure, and arrived at judgments that were slow but almost always correct. His tutors noted that he lacked charm and subtlety (observations that would follow him through his career) but that his commitment to the process of truth-finding was absolute. He was ordained as a Hem'Netjer at twenty-two, assigned first to minor judicial duties at Ankh-Setûn, and over the following years he built a reputation as a careful, thorough, and incorruptible temple judge.

At twenty-eight, he was transferred to the great Temple of Má'át at Zerula — a significant promotion. Zerula is the scholarly and cultural heart of the empire, and its Má'át temple serves as the supreme judicial authority for the surrounding nomes. The temple adjudicates boundary disputes, inheritance claims, commercial fraud, and — most importantly — administers the sacred oaths that bind the empire's legal system. When a nomarch swears fealty, when a merchant seals a contract under divine witness, when a condemned man appeals his sentence, it is a priest of Má'át who presides. The temple's judgments carry the weight of cosmic law, and the Wer'Hekau of Má'át at Zerula — a man named Senusret — was one of the most powerful religious figures outside the capital.

Hotep served at Zerula for nine years. He rose steadily: first conducting minor arbitrations, then presiding over commercial disputes between trading houses, then serving on the judicial panels that heard appeals from provincial courts. He was respected by his peers for his thoroughness and his unwillingness to take shortcuts. He was not loved. Hotep has never been easy company — he is serious where others are convivial, precise where others are approximate, and possessed of a moral certainty that can feel like judgment even when none is intended. But in a temple devoted to truth, his qualities were valued even when they were not enjoyed.

The trouble began in the seventh year of his posting, when Hotep was assigned to audit the temple's oracular records — the logs of auguries, divine pronouncements, and ritual verdicts delivered by the senior Hem'Netjer on behalf of the goddess. It was routine work, part of the temple's internal quality procedures. The records were supposed to be consistent: each augury logged with the ritual conditions, the question posed, the signs observed, and the interpretation rendered. Hotep found discrepancies. Small ones, at first — dates that didn't align with the ritual calendar, interpretations that referenced signs not recorded in the observation logs. He flagged them as clerical errors. But as he dug deeper, the pattern sharpened. A series of major auguries delivered to the nome's wealthiest families over the preceding five years had observation logs that were internally inconsistent — the signs described in the records did not match the astronomical conditions on the dates listed. Either the rituals had been conducted on different dates than claimed, or the signs had been fabricated entirely.

The implications were staggering. If the auguries had been manufactured, then the judgments based on them — land grants, inheritance rulings, commercial arbitrations worth enormous sums — were fraudulent. Not merely mistaken, but deliberately corrupted. And the auguries in question had all been conducted by the temple's most senior Hem'Netjer, priests who reported directly to Wer'Hekau Senusret.

Hotep spent six months quietly documenting the discrepancies, cross-referencing them with astronomical records maintained independently by the temple's Wab scholars (who tracked celestial observations for ritual planning, not judicial purposes). The evidence was damning. He compiled a formal report, following precisely the procedures his training prescribed, and presented it to Wer'Hekau Senusret directly — believing, with the earnest faith of a principled man, that the Wer'Hekau would be horrified and would act to purge the corruption.

Senusret was not horrified. He had authorized the fabrications himself.

The corrupt auguries had been part of an arrangement between the temple and Haty'a Nebmaatre, the nomarch of Zerula's nome. The nomarch provided the temple with land grants, construction funds, and political protection; in return, the temple's oracular pronouncements consistently favored the nomarch's allies, validated his territorial claims against rival nomarchs, and blessed his commercial ventures with divine sanction. It was a partnership of mutual enrichment disguised as sacred duty — precisely the kind of corruption that the theology of Má'át was supposed to make impossible.

What followed was swift and brutal. Within a week of presenting his report, Hotep was arrested by the temple's own judicial officers — priests he had served alongside for years. The charge was not corruption or whistleblowing; it was heresy. Specifically: blasphemous denial of the goddess's voice. In Kemetían theological law, the Wer'Hekau speaks with the authority of the deity. To accuse a Wer'Hekau of fabricating divine pronouncements is, doctrinally, to claim that the goddess has abandoned her own temple — a statement so dangerous to the religious order that it constitutes an attack on the divine itself. The charge was designed to be unanswerable. If Hotep was right about the corruption, then Má'át had failed to protect her own house, which meant the cosmic order was broken — a claim no sane priest would make. If he was wrong, he had slandered the goddess's representative. Either way, he was guilty.

The trial lasted one day. Hotep's evidence was confiscated and sealed in the temple archives. His name was struck from the rolls of the ordained. The feather tattoos on his forearms — the marks of his ordination that he had carried for eighteen years — were ritually defaced with a blade, and a brand of expulsion was burned into his left palm. He was escorted to the city gates and told that if he was found within Zerula's nome again, the sentence would be death.

During the years before his fall, Hotep had maintained a friendship with Nebet Hathura Ise'fré, the Wer'Hekau of Má'át in the capital — his own order's supreme authority. They had met when Hotep traveled to the capital as part of a judicial delegation from Zerula, and something between them clicked immediately: two sharp-minded, serious priests who recognized in each other an uncommon commitment to the principle they served. Their friendship was built on intellectual respect and a shared impatience with the performative aspects of temple politics; Hathura read motive where Hotep read evidence, and their conversations sharpened both. He considered her the finest legal mind in the order. She considered him the most principled judge she had ever met. In a profession full of political operators, each found in the other someone who seemed genuinely devoted to truth.

When the heresy charge was brought, the case should have been escalated to Hathura for review — as Wer'Hekau of Má'át in the capital, she held nominal supervisory authority over every Má'át temple in the empire, including Zerula's. Senusret moved too quickly, invoking emergency theological provisions to handle the matter locally before word reached the capital. By the time Hathura learned of the charges, the trial was concluded and the sentence carried out. She could still have demanded a formal review. She chose not to. Her reasons were sound by every political measure: challenging Senusret publicly would have split the Má'át order between the capital and the provinces, exposed the judicial system to a crisis of legitimacy, and handed her enemies — the Wer'Hekau of Rā chief among them — the evidence they needed to argue that Má'át's house was not in order. One man's exile was a tragedy. A schism in the order of truth would have been a catastrophe.

Hotep does not know the full extent of what she could have done. He knows she held authority over Zerula. He knows she did nothing. Whether she chose silence out of political calculation, genuine uncertainty about his claims, or something more complicated — an awareness that investigating Zerula might reveal the limits of her own oversight across the provincial temples — is the question that eats at him. He does not hate her. He is not certain he has the right to. But the silence of the one person who could have saved him is the wound that has healed the least.

The three years since his exile have been hard. Hotep wandered south along the river, then east into the hill country, surviving on the skills he had and the ones he acquired. His metalworking — a craft he had learned years ago from temple artisans who maintained the ritual bronzework and which he had always practiced as a meditative discipline — became his livelihood. He repaired tools, sharpened blades, and mended pots in the villages he passed through, earning enough to eat and move on. His staff-work, originally trained in the temple's judicial enforcement corps (the armed priests who enforced court orders and protected the temple's judicial proceedings), kept him alive on the roads. He is not a warrior, but he is a large man with training and a staff, and the roads of the empire are not as dangerous for him as they might be for a scholar.

He met Meret-Sebek Heka'amun in the hill country east of Zerula, roughly a year into his exile. The astrologer was already on his sacred journey, pursuing the celestial disturbances he called the Convergence of the Nine Moons, and Hotep felt the wrongness of it before Meret-Sebek explained it — a deep, visceral sense that the cosmic order was under strain, the same faculty that had guided his work as a temple judge. He recognized that Meret-Sebek's quest was not merely astronomical but theological: something was disrupting the balance that Má'át held together, and restoring it was precisely the kind of work that a priest of truth and order was born to do. He joined the company not as a follower but as a companion, offering his knowledge of ritual procedure, his understanding of temple politics, and his willingness to walk into places that frightened other men. He serves as the group's spiritual anchor, the one who insists on doing things properly even when expediency beckons — a quality that Meret-Sebek, the visionary, needs more than he always admits.

What Hotep does not say, and what only those who watch him closely might suspect, is that he has not entirely given up on his faith. The temple expelled him. The priests who serve Má'át branded him a heretic. But Hotep's devotion was never to the temple as an institution — it was to the principle the temple claimed to serve. Truth does not stop being true because the people responsible for upholding it have failed. He still prays, alone and in silence, and he still carries a small set of consecrated ritual implements hidden in his pack: the bronze scales, the feather token, the chalk for inscribing the circle of judgment. He performs no public rites — he has no authority to do so, and the sentence of heresy means that any ritual he conducts is doctrinally void. But in the privacy of his own practice, between himself and the goddess he still believes in, Hotep Anu remains a priest.

## Psyche

### Personality

Hotep is serious, methodical, and possessed of a moral certainty that most people find either admirable or exhausting, depending on how long they have spent in his company. He does not make small talk well. He does not laugh easily. He speaks with the deliberate precision of a man trained to weigh every word against the standard of truth, and this habit — useful in a courtroom, wearying at a campfire — gives his conversation a weight that can feel like judgment even when he intends none. He is aware of this effect and has largely stopped trying to correct it. People will think what they think; what matters is whether he has spoken truly.

Beneath the gravity, there is genuine warmth — but it is the warmth of a man who expresses care through action rather than words. He cooks for the group without being asked, takes watch without complaint, mends gear with steady hands, and will walk into danger for a companion without hesitation or fanfare. He does not say "I value you." He makes sure you eat, sleep safely, and survive.

His exile has hardened him in some ways and softened him in others. The temple trained him to be certain; the road taught him that certainty has costs, and that the people who pay those costs are not always the ones who earned them. He is less rigid than he was — more willing to consider that a situation might have dimensions he hasn't seen, more patient with people who make choices he finds difficult to respect. But the core has not shifted. He believes that truth matters more than comfort, that justice is not a negotiation, and that the cosmic order he devoted his life to serving is real even if the institution that claimed to represent it has betrayed it. He will not compromise on these convictions, and he does not expect others to share them.

### Motivation

Hotep's primary drive is restoration — not of his personal reputation, though he would not refuse it, but of the truth. The corruption in the Temple of Má'át at Zerula is not merely an institutional scandal; it is a wound in the cosmic order, a place where the principle of truth has been deliberately broken by the people entrusted to uphold it. As long as Senusret's fabricated auguries stand unchallenged, the judgments based on them remain in force — land was redistributed, families were dispossessed, commercial disputes were settled on the basis of lies spoken in the goddess's name. Hotep cannot undo this alone, but he cannot stop believing it must be undone.

His attachment to Meret-Sebek's quest is genuine but entangled with this deeper purpose. The Convergence is a cosmic disturbance, and Hotep believes that cosmic disturbances do not arise in isolation — they are symptoms of accumulated disorder, fractures in the fabric that Má'át holds together. Whether the corruption at Zerula is connected to the celestial instability or merely another expression of the same underlying rot, he does not know. But he knows that walking toward the disturbance is the right direction, and that is enough.

There is also a quieter motivation, one he would deny if asked directly: he wants to see Hathura again. Not for romance — their friendship was never that — but because she is the only person of genuine power who might believe him, and because her silence during his trial is a question he needs answered. Did she choose politics over principle, or did she genuinely doubt him? The answer matters to him far more than it should.

### Strengths

Hotep's foundational strength is his training in the judicial procedures of Má'át. He can evaluate evidence, identify inconsistencies in testimony, and construct arguments with a rigor that most people find impossible to match. When the group encounters conflicting accounts, hidden motives, or deliberate deception, Hotep is the one who sits quietly, asks precise questions, and assembles the truth from fragments that others overlooked. This is not intuition — it is method, drilled into him over decades, and it functions as reliably as any skill he possesses.

His command presence is considerable. Despite his exile, Hotep carries the bearing of a man who spent years presiding over judicial proceedings and directing temple enforcement operations. When he speaks with authority, people tend to listen — not because of his current station (which is nothing) but because something in his voice and manner communicates that he is accustomed to being obeyed and has earned the right. His knowledge of Kemetían religious and political systems — temple hierarchies, noble houses, legal precedent, ritual protocol — makes him an invaluable guide through the empire's institutional landscape.

Physically, he is the most capable combatant in Meret-Sebek's company, though he would not describe himself that way. His staff training from the temple's judicial enforcement corps was practical and thorough — designed to subdue, restrain, and if necessary incapacitate people who resisted the temple's legal authority — and three years on the road have kept him sharp. He is not a soldier, but he is a large, strong, well-trained man who does not panic under pressure and who has learned the hard way that the road does not care about your theological principles.

His metalworking skill, learned from the temple's ritual artisans and refined during exile, provides both practical utility and a source of income. He can repair weapons, mend armor, and fabricate simple tools — capabilities that make the group significantly more self-sufficient on extended journeys.

### Weaknesses

Hotep's most dangerous weakness is his rigidity. He has spent his life in service to an absolute principle — truth is truth, regardless of consequences — and this commitment, which made him a superb temple judge, makes him a difficult companion and a predictable adversary. He struggles with situations that require compromise, strategic deception, or the kind of moral flexibility that the real world constantly demands. Meret-Sebek and the others have learned to work around this, but there are moments when Hotep's insistence on doing things the right way has cost the group time, opportunities, and goodwill.

His empathy is genuinely limited. He is not cruel — he takes no pleasure in others' pain — but he often fails to recognize emotional dimensions of situations that are obvious to everyone else. He approaches interpersonal problems as if they were legal disputes, looking for facts and procedures when what people need is understanding. This blind spot has strained his relationships throughout his life and contributed to his isolation after exile. The temple valued his thoroughness; outside the temple, thoroughness without warmth is just coldness with better documentation.

He carries deep anger — not the hot, expressive kind, but the cold, settled kind that accumulates over years of injustice. Anger at Senusret, at the priests who knew and said nothing, at the system that punished truth-telling and rewarded complicity. He controls it well, but it surfaces in moments of stress as a biting harshness that can wound the people closest to him. He is aware of it and ashamed of it, which does not stop it from happening.

Finally, his exile has left him without institutional support of any kind. He has no wealth, no patron, no temple authority, no legal standing. In a civilization where identity is bound to institutional affiliation — where a priest without a temple is barely a person — Hotep is functionally a nonentity. Any encounter with imperial authority is a risk; if his identity were discovered by the wrong people, the sentence of death that awaits him in Zerula's nome could be extended to other jurisdictions.

## Social

### Patrons

- **Meret-Sebek Heka'amun**: The astrologer is Hotep's closest companion and the nearest thing he has to a patron. Meret-Sebek provides the quest that gives Hotep's exile purpose, and in return Hotep provides the spiritual grounding and institutional knowledge that Meret-Sebek lacks. Their relationship is one of mutual respect between equals, though the outside world sees Hotep as the astrologer's bodyguard, which he tolerates without correcting.

- **Hem'Netjer Kamenwati**: A junior priest at the Má'át temple in Ankh-Setûn — Hotep's home city and the temple where he began his career. Kamenwati was Hotep's student during his earliest years as an ordained Hem'Netjer and has never believed the heresy charge. He is too junior and too cautious to act openly, but he occasionally sends messages through intermediaries — warnings about temple politics, fragments of information about Senusret's activities, and quiet reassurance that not everyone in the order has forgotten what Hotep stood for. Kamenwati is terrified that his correspondence will be discovered.

- **Nebet Hathura Ise'fré** (estranged): The Wer'Hekau of Má'át in the capital — Hotep's own superior in the temple hierarchy, and once his closest friend within the order. Her silence during his trial severed the relationship, but Hotep has not given up entirely on the possibility that she might be reached. She is the only person with both the institutional authority to reopen his case and the legal acumen to understand the evidence he compiled. As Wer'Hekau, she could demand a formal review of the Zerula proceedings, subpoena the sealed archives, and — if the evidence holds — strip Senusret of his office. Whether she would risk the stability of the entire Má'át order to vindicate one branded heretic, even one she once called a friend, is the question Hotep has not yet found the courage to test.

### Enemies

- **Wer'Hekau Senusret**: The high priest of Má'át at Zerula and the architect of Hotep's destruction. Senusret is a pragmatist who views the temple's judicial authority as a political instrument to be wielded strategically rather than a sacred trust to be guarded absolutely. He is not a monster — he believes the arrangements with Haty'a Nebmaatre serve the temple's long-term interests and that the fabricated auguries are a necessary accommodation to political reality. He regards Hotep not as a hero but as a naive zealot whose rigid principles nearly brought down an institution that has served the goddess for centuries. He would prefer that Hotep remain forgotten, but if the exile resurfaces with renewed accusations, Senusret will act decisively to silence him.

- **Haty'a Nebmaatre**: The nomarch of Zerula's nome, the secular half of the corrupt partnership that Hotep exposed. Nebmaatre has benefited enormously from the fabricated auguries — territory, commercial monopolies, and political leverage all rest on judgments that Hotep's evidence could overturn. He maintains agents in the surrounding nomes who watch for any sign that the exiled priest is gathering support or evidence. Unlike Senusret, Nebmaatre views Hotep as an active threat and would be content to see him dead rather than merely silenced.

- **Hem'Netjer Irenakht**: A senior priest at Zerula who was directly involved in fabricating the augury records and who served as the chief prosecutor during Hotep's heresy trial. Irenakht is Senusret's enforcer — intelligent, ruthless, and deeply invested in ensuring that the corruption remains buried. He is the one most likely to recognize Hotep if the exile returns to the region.

### Affiliations

- **Meret-Sebek's Company**: Hotep travels with the astrologer Meret-Sebek Heka'amun, the scholar Neferet Imi-Ra, and the ranger Atenheru. The company is small, self-sufficient, and bound together by the shared purpose of the Convergence quest. Hotep functions as the group's spiritual guide, legal advisor, and — when necessary — its most capable fighter.

- **The Faithful Remnant**: A handful of scattered priests and former Wab throughout the empire who either knew Hotep personally or who have heard enough about the circumstances of his exile to doubt the official story. They do not constitute an organized network — most do not know each other — but they represent a potential source of shelter, information, and moral support should Hotep ever attempt to rebuild his case. Kamenwati in Ankh-Setûn is the most active, but there are others: a retired Hem'Netjer in the delta who trained alongside Hotep, a Wab scholar who independently noticed discrepancies in the Zerula augury records before being transferred to a remote provincial temple.

## Plot Hooks

1. **The Sealed Archive**: Kamenwati sends word that the evidence Hotep compiled — the astronomical cross-references, the augury logs, the discrepancy analysis — was not destroyed as Senusret claimed. It was sealed in the restricted archives beneath the Zerula temple, classified as heretical material too dangerous to circulate but too thoroughly documented to simply burn. Retrieving it would vindicate Hotep, but it would require entering Zerula's nome (where the death sentence applies), penetrating the temple's archive security, and escaping before Irenakht's network closes around him. The question is whether any ally could be persuaded to help — and whether the evidence, even recovered, would matter against the combined weight of a Wer'Hekau and a nomarch.

2. **The Capital Gambit**: Hotep learns through Kamenwati that the Wer'Hekau of Má'át in the capital — Senusret's superior — is conducting a quiet review of judicial irregularities across the empire's Má'át temples, driven by complaints from nomarchs whose rivals benefited from suspiciously favorable auguries. If Hotep could reach the capital and present his case directly, he might find a sympathetic ear at the highest level of his order. But reaching the capital means passing through hostile territory, and approaching the supreme Wer'Hekau as a branded heretic is as likely to end in re-arrest as in vindication. The gambit would be considerably safer with a powerful intermediary — someone like the Wer'Hekau of Íšét, who has the Per-Aá's ear and who once called Hotep a friend.

3. **The Old Friend**: The company's travels bring them within reach of the capital, and Hotep faces the decision he has been postponing: whether to seek out Hathura Ise'fré. She has the political power to help him, the intelligence to understand his evidence, and the institutional connections to get his case before the right people. But she also chose silence when he needed her most, and he does not know whether she would risk her position now — or whether approaching her would endanger them both. The complication deepens if Hathura is simultaneously dealing with her own crises (the spy in her circle, the rival Wer'Hekau, the heresy within her temple) and sees Hotep's reappearance as either an opportunity for alliance or a catastrophic liability.

4. **The Second Witness**: During the company's journey, they encounter a woman who claims to have served as a Wab in the Zerula temple during the period of the fabricated auguries. She was transferred abruptly to a remote provincial posting after asking questions about record-keeping irregularities — questions similar to the ones Hotep asked. She has her own partial evidence, independently gathered, that corroborates his claims. But she is frightened, reluctant to act, and deeply suspicious of Hotep's motives: the temple told her he was a heretic and a madman, and three years of believing that story is not easily overcome. Winning her trust — and protecting her from Senusret's agents, who are watching for exactly this kind of contact — becomes both a practical challenge and a test of Hotep's ability to connect with someone on an emotional level rather than a purely evidentiary one.

5. **The Scales of Judgment**: Meret-Sebek's quest leads the company to a sacred site where an ancient ritual of Má'át must be performed — a genuine weighing of truth, a ceremony so old that its power derives from the site itself rather than from any institutional authority. Hotep is the only member of the company with the ritual training to conduct it, but doing so would mean performing a sacred rite as a branded heretic — an act that his own theology condemns as void at best and blasphemous at worst. The theological question cuts to the heart of everything Hotep believes: Is his priesthood a function of institutional authority (which he has lost) or of personal devotion to truth (which he has not)? If he performs the rite and it works, it would be the most powerful evidence possible that the goddess has not abandoned him. If it fails, he will have proven the temple right.
