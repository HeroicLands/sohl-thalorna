# The Old Way

- **Pantheon:** [[affiliation-firstgods|The First Gods]]
- **Principal adherents:** [[doc-aelendnppl|Áelendan]]
- **Ancestral origin:** [[doc-pelwarpepl|Pelwar People]]

The **Old Way** is the shamanic faith of the Áelendan. It is the oldest continuously practised religious tradition on [[doc-ankrscntnnt|Ankaris Continent]] — a tradition that was already ancient when the ancestors of the Áelendan settled the mountainous interior of [[doc-elavendre|Élavendre]], and that has been preserved, generation by generation, by the tribal communities who carried it. Where other Pelwar peoples set the old faith aside as they built kingdoms and adopted the organised pantheons of the settled world, the Áelendan kept it.

The Old Way has no scripture, no temples in the sense that the [[affiliation-arldnpnthn|Aurèldían]] clergy would recognise, and no centralised hierarchy. Its teachings are carried in song, in ceremony, in the relationships between the tribes and the land they inhabit, and in the accumulated lore of the shamen.

## Cosmology

The Old Way's cosmology is not a catalogue of named gods with defined portfolios. It is a single great antinomy: the [[affiliation-firstgods#The Bright|Bright]] and the [[affiliation-firstgods#The Dark|Dark]], the two primordial Powers of the [[affiliation-firstgods|The First Gods]] locked in perpetual struggle. From their struggle arises the turning of the world — the alternation of day and night, of summer and winter, of growth and decay, of life and death. Neither Power is evil; neither is wholly good. Both are necessary. The world exists because they contend.

Between the Two Powers and mortal life stand the **[[affiliation-thekindred|Kindred]]** — the innumerable servant spirits of the First Gods. These are the spirits of animals, of groves, of rivers and lakes, of stones and peaks, of hearths and thresholds. Some serve the Bright, some the Dark, some both. They are the living presence of the First Gods in the mortal world, and they are the focus of active Áelendan religious practice — not as objects of worship, but as spirits to be **venerated, appeased, or warded against** as each relationship and circumstance requires.

This three-layered cosmology — Two Powers above, Kindred between, mortals below — is the basic map of the Old Way. It is not a cosmology of temples and altars. It is a cosmology of relationships.

## The Shamen

The religious specialists of the Áelendan are **shamen**, not priests. The distinction is not merely terminological. A priest of the Aurèldían Twelve is a trained intermediary who has been invested with authority by an institution — the temple, the chapterhouse, the formal clergy. A shaman of the Old Way has no institution behind them. They are a person who, by disposition and training, has learned to perceive the Kindred, to speak with them, and to negotiate on behalf of the tribe with the spirits whose territory the tribe shares.

Shamen are recognised by their tribes rather than ordained by any body. A child who shows signs of the perception — unusual rapport with animals, sensitivity to places, the capacity to dream true dreams — is taken as apprentice by an elder shaman, and the training proceeds over many years. Shamen learn the songs, the lineages of the Kindred in their tribe's territory, the protocols of offering and approach, the uses of fire and smoke and plant and water in the rites, and the long memory that holds the tribe's compact with each of its Kindred.

A tribe may have one shaman or several. Large tribes often have shamen of differing specialisations — one whose rapport is strongest with animal spirits, one with river-spirits, one with the spirits of the dead. The shamen of a tribe are regarded with deep respect but are not regarded as rulers; in most Áelendan tribes, political leadership sits with the elders and the council, and the shamen advise rather than command.

Shamen are expected to act on behalf of the tribe as a whole. Shamanic power turned to personal ends — cursing, private gain, coercion — is the gravest kind of religious offence the Áelendan recognise, and tribes have been known to exile shamen who transgress this boundary.

## Tribal Variation

The Old Way is not uniform across the Áelendan. It could not be. Each tribe's lived religion is shaped by the particular landscape it inhabits and the particular Kindred whose territories the tribe shares. A tribe of the high Grazian peaks keeps relationships with stone-warders and snow-spirits that mean little to a tribe of the lower forests, whose own dealings are with wolf-spirits and grove-keepers. A tribe seated beside a great lake maintains bonds with its river-mothers and lake-spirits that no inland tribe could claim.

Certain broad patterns are common to all Áelendan communities — the orientation toward the Two Powers, the reliance on shamen, the seasonal calendar, the rites of birth and passage and death — but the specific Kindred honoured, the specific songs sung, and the specific sites regarded as sacred vary from tribe to tribe. The Áelendan find this diversity unremarkable. The Kindred are of the land; naturally the tribes of different lands honour different Kindred.

## Veneration, Appeasement, and Warding

Áelendan religious practice does not take the form of worship in the sense that the Aurèldían or Asguardian faiths would recognise. The Áelendan do not petition their Powers; they do not sing hymns of praise to the Kindred; they do not beseech divine favour in the manner of a temple-goer before an altar. What they do is keep relationships, and those relationships take three broad forms — **veneration**, **appeasement**, and **warding** — which shamen and elders know to distinguish with precision.

**Veneration** is offered to the benevolent Kindred: the hearth-spirit who keeps the children warm through the winter, the river-mother whose waters are clean and whose fish are plentiful, the grove-keeper who permits the tribe to take its firewood and its game. Veneration takes the form of regular offerings, songs of thanks, and the maintenance of a good reputation in the spirit's sight. These are the easy relationships, and most Áelendan religious life is made of them.

**Appeasement** is offered to the severe or exacting Kindred: the stone-warder who keeps the high pass and demands particular silences, the threshold-spirit who exacts a price for safe crossing, the weather-kin who must be placated before a storm passes. Appeasement is a negotiated thing — the Kindred's requirements are specific, the offerings must be exactly what is asked, and the protocols must be followed without error. A shaman who mishandles an appeasement endangers the tribe.

**Warding** is the posture taken toward the dangerous, the capricious, or the frankly hostile: the old powers of the deep caves, the restless dead, certain fae-adjacent Kindred of the thin places, and a handful of particular spirits whose enmity a tribe has earned across generations. Warding is the use of ceremony, charm, fire, and avoidance to keep such beings at a distance. One does not bargain with what one wards against; one does not enter its territory without need; one carries the protective songs and the proper objects when one must.

Ceremonies are directed at specific Kindred rather than at the Two Powers. A hunt-rite addresses the wolf-spirit of a particular ridge, not the Bright or the Dark as such. A harvest-rite venerates the grove-keeper of a particular stand of trees. A funeral-rite commends the departed to the river-mother who will carry them on, or to the stone-warder who will keep their bones. The Two Powers stand behind all of these as the ultimate frame, but they are not themselves petitioned.

The Áelendan year is structured around a calendar of seasonal observances tied to the turning of the Two Powers — the solstices, the equinoxes, the liminal nights between them — and around the specific ceremonial occasions of each tribe's relationships with its own Kindred. These rites are not celebrations in the modern sense. They are the maintenance of the tribe's standing with the spirits whose lands they share.

Offerings are usually simple and specific to the recipient: food, drink, blood, breath, song, fire. The underlying principle is reciprocity. The Áelendan take from the land what the land offers; they give back what the Kindred have indicated, through long tradition, that they require.

## Relation to the Covenant

The Old Way is not the same thing as the **Covenant** — the ancient compact between the Áelendan and the land, the fae, and the older powers that dwell at the thin places. The Covenant is a specific sacred agreement whose terms are guarded by the [[affiliation-alndnwrdns|Wardens]]. The Old Way is the broader faith within which that compact is understood and maintained.

A shaman is not the same thing as a Warden, though individual Áelendan sometimes hold both roles. The shamen are concerned with the Kindred and with the tribes' ongoing religious life; the Wardens are concerned with the physical integrity of the sacred sites and the enforcement of the Covenant's terms. The two offices work together, but they are distinct.

Many of the [[doc-faecourts|Fae]] whom the Wardens deal with are understood, within the Old Way, as particularly ancient and powerful Kindred — though this is a matter on which shamen speak carefully and only to other shamen.

## Relation to Later Faiths

The Aurèldían pantheon has been present in Élavendre for many centuries and is the official faith of the Élavendri crown and nobility. The Áelendan do not object to it, and they do not prohibit their own people from taking part in Aurèldían observances when circumstances require. Áelendan bards travel to Aurèldían courts, Áelendan envoys attend Aurèldían festivals, and Áelendan traders in the lowlands have no difficulty participating in the civic rites of the towns they visit.

But the Áelendan do not regard Aurèldían worship as an alternative to the Old Way. They regard it, as noted in [[affiliation-firstgods|their teaching on the First Gods]], as a later refraction of the older truth — not wrong, but partial. The Aurèldían gods are children of the First, and honouring a child is not the same as honouring the parent.

Among the Élavendri nobility, who claim Áelendan ancestry and whose own religious practice is "heavily inflected by older fae traditions," there are individuals and whole families who have quietly kept the Old Way alongside their public Aurèldían observance. This is tolerated by the Aurèldían clergy, who treat it as charming ancestral piety; it is regarded with more suspicion by the [[affiliation-ordoarcanis|Ordo Arcanis]], which regards any ungoverned religious practice as a category of problem.
