# Nyáluba Spirits

- **Parent pantheon:** [[affiliation-nyalbpnthn|Nyáluba Pantheon]]
- **People:** [[doc-nylbtrblntn|Nyáluba Tribal Nation]]
- **Region:** southern [[doc-xerathia|Xerathia]]

The **Nyáluba Spirits** are the living face of the [[affiliation-nyalbpnthn|Nyáluba faith]] in the world. They are the clan totems, the land-spirits, the water-spirits, the hunt-spirits, and the ancestor-spirits whose territories and lineages interleave with those of the Nyáluba peoples. They are not gods. They are not worshipped. They are, in the language of the tradition, **venerated, appeased, or warded against** — each according to its character and the need of the moment. The three postures are those of the shamans themselves, and the craft of Nyáluba religious practice consists, in large part, of knowing which posture a given spirit requires.

## Character

The Nyáluba Spirits are particular. They are rooted in specific places or specific lineages, and their power — which can be considerable — is local to the territory or the kin-line they inhabit. A hippopotamus-spirit who wards a particular stretch of river is powerful on that stretch and nowhere else. A baobab-spirit who holds the gathering-tree of a particular village is the keeper of that village and only that village. An ancestor-spirit attends to the descendants of the clan that remembers their name, and no other. The Spirits are not transcendent. They do not preside over domains of existence in the manner of the Aurèldían or Kemetían gods. They are of the world, and the Nyáluba deal with them as kinsmen — close or distant, beloved or feared — whose territories the clan shares.

A particular spirit may be generous and forgiving; another, in an adjacent territory, may be strict and exacting. Every spirit has its own temperament, its own protocols, its own requirements, and its own history of dealings with the clans whose lives it touches. Shamans and griots are trained across many years to know which spirits the clan deals with, what each of them accepts, how each of them must be approached, and what each of them will not tolerate.

## The Three Postures

Nyáluba religious life distinguishes three standing postures toward the Spirits, and a shaman's craft consists, in large part, of knowing which posture each spirit requires and which posture a particular situation calls for.

- **Venerate** — The benevolent spirits are venerated: honoured with regular offerings, greeted at the seasons, and sung of in the clan's memory-songs. A river-mother whose waters are clean, a baobab-spirit who keeps the gathering-tree, an ancestor whose counsel still guides the clan, the hearth-spirit of the family compound — these are venerated. Most of Nyáluba religious life consists of such relationships, and the clan's reputation in the sight of its venerated spirits is the foundation of its standing.
- **Appease** — The severe, exacting, or easily offended spirits are appeased. Their specific demands are met; their protocols are followed without error; their offerings are rendered exactly as tradition requires. A crocodile-kin of a dangerous crossing, a drought-spirit who must be placated before the dry season, the spirit of a ridge where a clan elder was struck by lightning — these are appeased. Appeasement is negotiated, precise, and unforgiving of mistakes.
- **Ward against** — The dangerous, the capricious, and the outright hostile spirits are warded against: avoided where possible, sung away where not, kept outside the bounds of the clan's life by fire, charm, protective object, and the exact word of warding. Restless dead of cursed ground, the spirits of a place where a great wrong was done, the spirits of enmity earned by a rival clan in generations past — these are warded against. One does not bargain with what one wards against.

## Categories

The Nyáluba tradition does not impose a single rigid taxonomy on the Spirits, but several broad types recur across the clans:

- **Totems** — The ancestral or presiding spirits of a clan's totemic lineage. Not individual animals or objects, but the ancestral presence from which the clan traces its identity. Lion-totems, leopard-totems, elephant-totems, crocodile-totems, python-totems, eagle-totems, hyena-totems, baobab-totems, and many others. The totem is the clan's kinsman in the spirit world, and the clan's conduct is bound by the totem's nature.
- **Land-spirits** — Spirits whose domain is a specific place rather than a lineage. Baobab-keepers of particular gathering-trees, ridge-spirits of sacred heights, spirits of crossings, cairns, and thresholds. Many of the oldest Nyáluba sacred sites are tended by land-spirits who were ancient when the first clans settled the savannah.
- **Water-spirits** — Spirits of rivers, pools, waterfalls, and the great inland lakes. Often addressed as "mothers" (the river-mothers) or "fathers" (the crocodile-kin), depending on character. Water-spirits are consulted in matters of travel, crossing, drowning, fishing, and cleansing.
- **Hunt-spirits** — Spirits of particular game animals and particular hunting grounds. Addressed before the hunt, thanked after a kill, and offered a specific share of the take. A hunter who returns with full hands but no offering to the hunt-spirit of the ground is understood to have broken the standing pact of his clan.
- **Weather-spirits** — Spirits of storm, rain, wind, and the sun. More mobile than the territorial spirits, and often regarded as less reliable. Addressed in specific circumstances — a storm approaching, a drought settling, a harvest in the balance — rather than on a standing basis.
- **Ancestor-spirits** — The spirits of named ancestors of the clan. Unlike most of the other Spirits, ancestor-spirits are personal to a specific lineage and known by name; a clan's genealogy is, in effect, the roll of its ancestor-spirits, and the griot who remembers the genealogy keeps the clan's connection to them alive.
- **Hearth-spirits** — Spirits of the household, the family compound, and the communal fire. Each Nyáluba household has its own hearth-spirit, and major village hearths have powerful hearth-spirits of long standing.

## Relationship with the Clans

Each Nyáluba clan maintains a **standing pact** with each of the Spirits whose territory or lineage overlaps with the clan's — a relationship of offering, ceremony, and restraint. These pacts are not written, because the Nyáluba do not write. They are held in the griot's memory and transmitted through the oral tradition of the clan.

A pact includes, at minimum: the Spirit's name (or the clan's name for them), the territory or lineage the Spirit holds, the ceremonies the Spirit expects, the offerings the Spirit accepts, the times of the year at which the Spirit must be honoured, and the conduct the Spirit forbids. A clan that keeps its pacts prospers; a clan that breaks them suffers in specific, attributable ways — a failed hunt, a drowning at a crossing, a child born still, a fire that takes a compound. These outcomes are not regarded by the Nyáluba as superstition. They are regarded as consequence.

Pacts are inherited across generations, and the maintenance of them is one of the central obligations of the clan's griots, elders, and shamans. When a clan moves — through migration, war, or environmental change — new pacts must be formed with the Spirits of the new territory, and this is understood to be perilous work that can take generations to settle.

## Outsider Interaction

Outsiders — traders, travellers, foreign envoys — do not have pacts with the Nyáluba Spirits and cannot form them. What outsiders can do is refrain from transgression: not desecrating a sacred grove, not hunting what they have not been permitted to hunt, not entering a totemic precinct without invitation, not disturbing a cairn or a river-shrine. Most Nyáluba clans are willing to extend this minimum reciprocity to respectful visitors. Those who transgress discover, sometimes slowly and sometimes quickly, that the Spirits take offence, and that the clans do not intercede on behalf of a transgressor.
