God of wildness, unpredictability, and primal energy. Karnavos represents the untamed force of nature and the chaotic change necessary for growth.

## Worship

The worship of Karnavos is neither celebrated openly nor forbidden—it exists in a liminal space within Aureldian society, acknowledged by scholars and theologians but practiced in private by those who understand the god's necessity. There are no grand public temples dedicated to Karnavos in civilized cities; instead, his worship occurs at thresholds, boundaries, and wild places: crossroads shrines at the edge of towns, standing stones at the borders of forests, isolated mountain peaks, and the margins of cities where civilization fades into wilderness.

The faith teaches that Karnavos must be honored at certain critical moments—when new ventures begin and the old order must be shattered, when chaos brings necessary transformation, when rigid systems have become stagnant and must be broken apart to allow renewal. Merchants embarking on uncertain voyages, adventurers seeking fortune, revolutionaries plotting against tyranny, and philosophers questioning established truths may quietly invoke Karnavos in these liminal hours. The god does not demand submission or obedience but rather acknowledges the wild impulses that drive mortals to transgress boundaries and seek change.

During certain seasonal transitions—particularly at the boundaries between seasons, or at midnight on the new moon—small, private rites honor Karnavos at wilderness shrines. These ceremonies involve invoking the god's chaotic energy, acknowledging the disruptive forces that prevent the world from becoming too rigid and ordered. Offerings are left at night: wild meat, untamed plants, things that have resisted cultivation. Some faithful practice ecstatic rituals in secret groves, with dancing, drumming, and the deliberate breaking of taboos as expressions of Karnavos's wild blessing. These rites attract those who feel constrained by civilization and seek liberation, though the priesthood teaches that such liberation must be temporary and ultimately balanced by return to order.

Karnavos has no temples in the conventional sense, but many cities maintain small, hidden shrines—sometimes barely more than an altar stone hidden in a forest grove or behind a tavern. These are places of last resort, where the desperate or defiant may leave offerings and prayers when no god of order will answer them.

## Organization

Unlike the structured hierarchies of other Aureldian faiths, the priesthood of Karnavos is deliberately decentralized and anarchic. There is no High Priestess or universal priesthood; instead, local shamans, hermits, eccentric philosophers, and wilderness dwellers serve as informal priests of the god. These individuals are rarely formally ordained and often function as solitary practitioners rather than members of any institutional church.

What passes for organization among Karnavos's faithful is the **Circle of the Threshold**—a loose, constantly shifting fellowship of priests and philosophers who meet only occasionally at boundary places, usually at seasonal transitions. These gatherings involve ritual exchange of knowledge, debate about the god's will, and coordination of the few temples that do exist. No single figure holds ultimate authority; instead, decisions emerge from rough consensus among the assembled priests, and even then, individual practitioners remain free to interpret the god's teachings as they see fit.

The rank most important within Karnavos's informal priesthood is the **Hermit of Chaos**, an individual recognized for spiritual insight and connection to wild forces who serves as an oracle and teacher. These hermits are few and deeply respected within the faith, though they maintain no official authority and may have no followers at all. Some Hermits of Chaos live alone in the wilderness for decades; others wander cities as wandering holy fools or philosophers, speaking in riddles and challenging conventional thinking.

**Acolytes of the Threshold** are younger practitioners training in wilderness survival, shamanic techniques, and the theological understanding of chaos. However, unlike acolytes of other faiths, they may be largely self-taught, learning from books and solitary practice rather than formal training. The faith trusts that those truly called to Karnavos will find their own way to understanding.

Because organized temples are few and maintained with difficulty in civilized lands, some cities support hidden shrine-keepers—priests who maintain small, secret altars and conduct quiet ceremonies far from the authorities' eyes. These individuals often live double lives, publicly worshipping more conventional deities while secretly tending Karnavos's worship.

## Relations

The relationship between Karnavos and [[affiliation-janus|Jánus]] is one of deep, fundamental opposition. Jánus demands order, law, contracts, and hierarchical structure; Karnavos embodies the unpredictable forces that shatter boundaries and undo careful plans. Jánite authorities in many kingdoms actively suppress Karnavos's worship, viewing him as a threat to civilized order. Yet Jánite theology acknowledges Karnavos's reality—the god cannot be denied, only restrained. This creates a tense, grudging coexistence: Jánus and Karnavos are locked in eternal struggle, with mortals caught between them.

Karnavos is courted by [[affiliation-blackflame|The Black Flame]], which sees in him a kindred spirit of liberation and transgression. The heretical fire-cult seeks to recruit Karnavos's priests and to blend worship of the chaos god with their own destructive philosophy. However, the mainstream Karnavic priesthood—what little exists of it—largely rejects this alliance as a corruption of their god's nature. Karnavos represents necessary, transformative chaos; the Black Flame represents pure destruction for its own sake. The distinction is subtle but crucial to those who understand.

The relationship with [[affiliation-lusinia|Lúsinía]] is one of necessary tension. Where Lúsinía represents nature brought into order through cultivation and gardening, Karnavos represents wild, untamed nature—forests growing thick and dark, rivers breaking their banks, seasons shifting unpredictably. Lúsinía teaches that chaos must be balanced and organized; Karnavos teaches that organization can become stagnant and oppressive. The two deities are often invoked in paradox: storms destroy crops planted with Lúsinía's blessing, yet those storms bring soil renewal and create space for new growth that neither god could manage alone.

With [[affiliation-morvana|Mórváná]], Karnavos shares an understanding of transformation and change, though they approach it differently. Mórváná represents inevitable ending and natural decay; Karnavos represents the violent, chaotic disruption that tears down systems before their time. The two are sometimes invoked together at moments of profound transition—death combined with rebirth, destruction paired with renewal.

With all other Aureldian deities, Karnavos maintains a position of acknowledged but troubled coexistence. He is accepted as part of the divine cosmos, but also feared and often suppressed by the priesthoods of more orderly deities. Even [[affiliation-aetheria|Æthería]], the dreamer-goddess usually sympathetic to unconventional wisdom, regards Karnavos with caution—for dreams, however wild, eventually end and the dreamer wakes to order, while Karnavos seeks to shatter even that awakening.
