God of death, the underworld, and corruption. Thánatos is a fearsome figure, embodying both implacability and terror.

## Worship

The worship of Thánatos is conducted in whispers and shadows, never in the open exultation that characterizes the temples of other Aureldian deities. The faithful approach his shrines with a mixture of reverence and dread, understanding that to invoke the god of death is to acknowledge mortality itself. Yet Thánatos is not hated—rather, he is respected as an absolute and necessary force, the one truth that cannot be denied or negotiated: all mortals must eventually pass through his gate.

The most solemn festival of Thánatos occurs in late autumn, as the year wanes and life retreats from the land. During the Festival of the Silent Passage, which lasts three days and three nights, the temples close their doors to the living and the priesthood performs elaborate funerary rites for the dead of the past year whose families request it. As dusk falls, masked priests bearing torches process through city streets, and those present fall silent to honor the departed. In homes throughout the city, families set black candles in windows as a sign of remembrance, and the living speak the names of the dead aloud so that Thánatos might guide them safely through the underworld. Some faiths celebrate death; the Thánatic faithful instead acknowledge it with solemn ceremony—not celebrating, but affirming the god's sovereignty and submitting to his inevitable claim.

Individual devotion to Thánatos is largely private. Many who are gravely ill or who sense death approaching make pilgrimage to his temples in their final days, seeking absolution and peace. A priest of Thánatos will sit with the dying, reciting prayers that affirm the god's justice and the peace that awaits beyond the gate. The dying person speaks their final testimony, confessing wrongs and affirming goods, so that they may face Thánatos with an unburdened heart. This practice has given the Thánatic priesthood a unique role as counselor to the dying and keeper of deathbed confessions.

Offerings to Thánatos are sparse and austere—flowers that have withered, grain that has rotted, fruit past its ripeness. The symbolism is clear: life given back to the cycle, decay accepted as necessary. Some nobles maintain Thánatic shrines in family crypts and make annual offerings of wine poured upon the graves of ancestors, asking Thánatos to shelter their spirits and keep watch over their rest. The god accepts offerings made with genuine acceptance of death's inevitability, but rejects those made in desperate bargaining—Thánatos cannot be appeased into granting longer life.

## Organization

The priesthood of Thánatos is smaller and more secretive than those of other Aureldian deities, and its hierarchy is defined more by wisdom and philosophical depth than by administrative rank. At the apex stands the **Pontifex Mortis**, a title held by one among the Thánatic priests—rarely the same individual for more than a decade, as the role demands a level of existential contemplation that few can sustain indefinitely. The Pontifex serves as final arbiter of Thánatic doctrine and oversees the great temple complex where the most sacred rites are performed.

Below the Pontifex are the **Custodes Liminis**—the Threshold Keepers—a body of perhaps twenty to thirty ordained priests scattered throughout the major cities of western Ankaris. These priests are responsible for performing funeral rites, maintaining temple records of the dead, and counseling the dying. They are scholars of the underworld, experts in the theological mysteries of what lies beyond the gate. The Custodes Liminis are often consulted by nobility on matters of succession and inheritance, as they understand the theological implications of death and the transition of power.

The **Novices of the Silent Watch** form the lower tier of the priesthood—young men and women, often those who have survived near-death experiences and feel called to Thánatos's service. These novices undergo extensive training in funerary practice, theological study, and the maintenance of crypts and burial grounds. They serve as gravediggers, tomb-keepers, and assistant priests at funeral ceremonies. Many remain in this rank indefinitely, finding peace and purpose in the contemplative care of the dead.

The temples of Thánatos are typically located on the edges of cities—near cemeteries, in quiet groves, in locations of natural transition between the living world and the realm of death. The great temples employ custodians, record-keepers, and groundskeepers who maintain vast necropoli and libraries of the dead. Unlike the grand stone temples of Jánus or the Sacred Forge, Thánatic temples are often constructed with dark wood and worked stone in understated architectural styles, designed to blend with the landscape rather than dominate it. Their interiors are lit by oil lamps and filled with the scent of incense and myrrh, creating an atmosphere conducive to meditation on mortality.

The priesthood is funded through a combination of endowments from wealthy families maintaining private crypts, fees paid by those wishing proper funeral rites, and donations from the pious who wish to ensure their own deaths are handled with dignity. The temples maintain vast underground chambers where the honored dead are placed, and families may pay for the perpetual maintenance and care of their relatives' tombs. This system ensures the priesthood's independence from any kingdom or noble house, though the Thánatic priests are expected to perform final rites for paupers and the unclaimed dead without payment.

## Relations

Thánatos exists in a strange partnership with [[affiliation-lusinia|Faith of Lúsinía]], the World-Weaver. Creation and death are complementary forces; without Thánatos's claim, mortals would fear the endless extension of their lives. The two deities together maintain the cycle of existence—Lúsinía weaves the thread, Thánatos cuts it at the appointed hour. Their priesthoods respect one another and coordinate in blessing newborns and the dying as they pass between the domains of the two gods.

The relationship between Thánatos and [[affiliation-morvana|Faith of Mórváná]] (Mistress of the Waning Moon) is one of close alliance. Decay and death are adjacent mysteries; where Thánatos judges and claims the soul, Mórváná governs the body's transformation into dust and return to the earth. The two priesthoods maintain quiet cooperation, honoring the dead and acknowledging the necessity of decomposition. Temples dedicated to both deities are sometimes constructed in proximity, creating sacred spaces where death and renewal are understood together.

Thánatos maintains a complex neutrality with [[affiliation-janus|Faith of Jánus]]. The god of order demands that deaths follow proper law and ceremony, and Thánatic priests ensure this standard is upheld. Yet where Jánus fights against chaos and decay, Thánatos accepts them as inevitable. This theological tension is managed rather than resolved—both priests understand they serve different aspects of a universal order, neither superior to the other.

The faith of Thánatos stands in clear opposition to any theology that promises to defeat or circumvent death itself. Necromancers, those who raise the dead, and any practice that violates the sanctity of the grave offend Thánatos directly. The priesthood maintains no formal alliance with other faiths on this matter, but they are universally respected for their determination to preserve the dignity of death and the finality of Thánatos's judgment. Even the followers of [[affiliation-blackflame|The Black Flame]], despite their general contempt for the order-centered faiths, respect Thánatos as a primal force that cannot be bargained with or overcome through mere strength of will.
