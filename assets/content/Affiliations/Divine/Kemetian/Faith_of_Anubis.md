Ánubís is the god of decay, mummification, transformation, and the compassionate guidance of the dead through the most fundamental of all transitions—the passage from life to death and beyond. To most mortal minds, decay and putrefaction evoke revulsion and fear; they are processes to be denied, avoided, and overcome. But in Kheperi theology, the insight runs deeper: decay is not meaningless annihilation but rather a necessary and sacred transformation. The body that has served its owner faithfully during life must be transformed before the spirit can progress into eternity. Ánubís presides over this transformation, ensuring it occurs with reverence, precision, and compassionate respect for the dead.

The symbol of Ánubís—a jackal's head cradling a crescent moon—carries profound theological weight. The jackal is a creature native to the scrublands and wild margins that flank the Kheperi countryside, a scavenger and survivor that moves through the night with quiet dignity. In Kheperi understanding, the jackal is not a defiler but rather a participant in natural cycles, a creature that understands the necessity of returning bodies to the earth. The crescent moon symbolizes night itself and also renewal, the cyclical transformation by which the old becomes the new. Together, they represent a deity who is neither reviled nor feared—as Ápōphis might be—but rather honored as performing a necessary and inherently beautiful function.

In practice, Ánubís is honored far more universally than many other deities because essentially every human being will eventually require the god's services. Birth comes under the domain of Íšét; death comes under the domain of Sēker; but it is Ánubís who manages the sacred intermediate state. The Kheperi have developed an entire industrial civilization around death—the mummification process, the preparation of tombs, the creation of burial goods, the composition of funerary texts. All of this complex apparatus exists under Ánubís's auspices, and the priesthood of this god is correspondingly vast, wealthy, and influential.

What distinguishes Ánubís from deities of death like Sēker is the specificity of domain. Sēker presides over the spiritual journey of the dead, guiding souls through the complex trials and passages of the underworld. Ánubís focuses on the physical preparation and transformation. The precise ritual of mummification—the removal of organs, the drying and preservation of the body, the wrapping in linen and precious oils, the placement of amulets and protective spells—all of this is Ánubís's sacred work. A master embalmer is considered a devoted servant of Ánubís, performing sacred priesthood through their skilled hands. The knowledge required to preserve a body perfectly against decay for millennia is understood not as mere craft but as a form of divine service.

The transformation that Ánubís enables is genuinely loving in nature. The god does not torture the dead or punish them; rather, Ánubís ensures that each corpse is treated with dignity, that nothing of value is wasted, that the dead are prepared for their eternal rest with meticulous care. In this regard, Ánubís is one of the most beloved deities in the Kheperi pantheon, because nearly all mortals will eventually encounter this god not as a threat but as a compassionate guide. To trust in Ánubís is to trust that one's final days will be handled with respect and care, that one's body will not be abandoned to decay in wilderness or desecration but rather will be lovingly transformed into imperishable form.

The cultural obsession with death and the afterlife that defines Kheperi civilization is ultimately rooted in faith in Ánubís. Kheperi dedicate enormous resources to tomb-building and burial preparation because they believe that Ánubís's transformation of the corpse is not the end of their existence but rather the transition point into eternal continuation. The vast necropoli that surround great Kheperi cities are not monuments to death so much as monuments to the belief that Ánubís will preserve them, that the god's art will keep them whole and incorruptible, and that they will dwell in those preserved bodies eternally.

## Worship

The worship of Ánubís is most intensely practiced in the context of death itself. When a person dies, the family immediately brings the body to an embalming temple, where priests and skilled craftspeople of Ánubís take custody of the remains. The family participates in rituals and prayers that commend their loved one to Ánubís's care, asking the god to guide the transformation and to ensure the spirit's successful passage into the afterlife.

The mummification process itself is a sacred ritual, every step performed with deliberate ceremony and accompanying prayers. As the embalmer removes the internal organs—a process that causes visible decay—the attending priest chants prayers to Ánubís, explaining and justifying each action. "Ánubís, Keeper of Transitions, we remove these organs not to harm but to preserve. We dry these flesh to prevent corruption. We anoint with these oils to honor the body that served this soul faithfully in life." This ritual language transforms what might otherwise seem like desecration into an act of profound respect.

The most important annual observance honoring Ánubís is the Festival of the Preserved Dead, celebrated during the height of the inundation season when agricultural work ceases and the land enters into a kind of hibernation. During this festival, priests perform ceremonies in the great necropoli, walking among the tombs and making offerings to the dead. Families visit the tombs of their ancestors, bringing food and drink, speaking aloud to the deceased, and asking Ánubís to continue protecting and preserving their beloved ones.

Beyond formal festivals, Ánubís worship is integrated into daily life through the use of amulets and charms for protection against decay and corruption. Nearly every household keeps small mummified objects—mice or birds preserved using techniques developed by priests of Ánubís—as talismans ensuring that one's body too will be perfectly preserved at the time of death. These amulets are often passed down through families for generations, and their continued integrity (never rotting, never decaying) is taken as reassuring evidence of Ánubís's power and benevolence.

## Organization

The priesthood of Ánubís is among the largest and most powerful in Ta'Kheperu, for the reasons stated above: essentially every human being eventually requires these priests' services, and the preparation of tombs and burial goods creates enormous economic activity. The Wer'Hekau (High Priest) of Ánubís typically wields influence second only to the Per-Aá themselves and may occasionally rival the Per-Aá's power, depending on the particular political alignments and the strength of individual priesthoods.

Below the High Priest, an extensive hierarchy of Hem'Netjer (ordained priests) manages the various specialized functions of Ánubís's domain. Some Hem'Netjer are primarily scholars and ritualists, chanting the required prayers and supervising complex ceremonies. Others are master embalmers, individuals who have spent decades perfecting the technical knowledge required to preserve bodies perfectly. Still others manage the vast facilities associated with mummification: the drying beds where bodies are treated with natron, the workshops where linen is prepared, the chambers where aromatic oils and ointments are created.

The Wab (acolytes) begin their training at young ages and typically spend years learning the technical and spiritual aspects of mummification. An acolyte might apprentice under a master embalmer, learning the precise anatomical knowledge and manual skills required. Alternatively, an acolyte might train primarily as a ritualist and scholar, studying the funerary texts and prayers. The most capable acolytes eventually rise to the rank of Hem'Netjer and take leadership positions within the priesthood.

A notable feature of Ánubís's priesthood is that family inheritance plays a significant role in priestly succession. Master embalmers often teach their craft to their children and grandchildren, creating family dynasties of religious specialists. This is permitted because the skills are understood as sacred knowledge, and a family's accumulated expertise is considered divinely bestowed. Some of the greatest embalming families in Ta'Kheperu have maintained their practices for generations, each generation adding refinements and innovations to the techniques passed down by their ancestors.

## Relations

Ánubís's relationship with Sēker, the Lord of Silent Passage, is complementary and deeply important in Kheperi theology. The two gods work together in the service of the dead: Ánubís transforms and preserves the body; Sēker guides the spirit through its trials and eventual rest. In temple theology, priests teach that the work of Ánubís and Sēker are inseparable—without Ánubís's careful preservation of the body, the spirit would have no anchor point for its eternal existence. Without Sēker's guidance of the spirit, even a perfectly preserved body would be meaningless.

Ánubís also maintains important relations with [[affiliation-iset|Faith of Íšét]], the goddess of healing and renewal. While Íšét works to preserve life and health in living bodies, Ánubís preserves bodies after death. The two goddesses are understood as bookends of physical existence: Íšét protects and heals during life; Ánubís preserves and transforms after death. Many temples maintain shrines to both deities, and priests teach that trust in Íšét during life and trust in Ánubís at death creates continuity and wholeness across the boundary between existence and eternity.

The relationship between Ánubís and Thōth, the god of wisdom and knowledge, is important because Thōth ensures that knowledge about mummification and funeral rites is preserved and improved over generations. Thōth's sacred ibis-quill has recorded countless refinements to Ánubís's sacred craft, and new innovations in embalming technique are understood as discoveries made possible by Thōth's blessing. Priests of both deities often work closely together, with Thōth's scholars documenting and analyzing the techniques of Ánubís's practitioners.

Finally, Ánubís maintains protective relations with Rá, the solar god. Rá's heat is understood as one of the forces that aids in the preservation process—sunlight and heat help dry the body before the formal application of natron. Priests make offerings to both Rá and Ánubís as part of the mummification ritual, thanking Rá for the preservative heat of his light and Ánubís for the skilled knowledge to use that heat most effectively.
